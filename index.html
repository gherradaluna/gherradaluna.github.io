About Me

“The greatest achievements in art and science, I believe, have been made by men, who had faith, or compassion for their fellow men”
— Gerty Cori

Welcome to my website! My name is Gilbert Herrada Luna. I grew up in a rural town called Hayesville in Western North Carolina to 1st generation Mexican immigrants. To support my family, my father had entered a career in construction, where he worked long hours with poor compensation.

Consequently, for as long as I can remember, my father returned home from work daily with a bag of potato chips. In his early twenties, as a result of his new chip-eating habit, a physician diagnosed my father with high blood pressure, informing him that he had one of the youngest cases of hypertension she had ever seen at the time. As such, despite my family’s pleas and his physician’s warning that they would worsen his hypertension, my father never stopped buying potato chips. A habit that, nearly two decades later, left him at high risk for coronary artery disease. His physician resultantly ordered a coronary artery calcium scan, advising him to reduce how frequently he consumed potato chips. Thus, my father was devastated by the news, as being an uninsured immigrant supporting a financially stretched family made him reluctant to spend money on his own healthcare.

In light of my father’s health concerns, my mother grew curious as to if other families had similar health habits or issues. Due to the fact that my mother spoke poor English, she took me along with her to ask her “white” friends if their families had similar health problems. My mother and I learned, in consequence of the cohesive nature of my community, that my father was not alone in his struggles, as his condition seemed to be common among the other families we had talked to. For this reason, I began to wonder why hypertension seemed so prevalent among those close to me. This hands-on experience therefore catalyzed my interest in healthcare, particularly regarding the therapeutic effects of nutrition. It was in light of my mother’s curiosity that prompted me to shift my perspective from an individualized view of health to a population-level framework.

Now, I attend the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill with the goal of studying Nutrition through the Gillings School of Public Health. Through this major, I intend to pursue coursework that will deepen my knowledge of the biomechanical mechanisms related to the metabolization of micronutrients and macronutrients. As an undergraduate, I hope to become involved with a lab associated with the UNC Nutrition Research Institute to explore not merely the corollary, but the causal relationship between nutrition and health, and the applications of this relationship to the community of Western North Carolina.

In the long term, while my exact path is uncertain, I hope to become a nutritionally competent clinician in a rural area such as Clay County, that has the skills to look at the health of an individual and of a population. Therefore, having seen firsthand how nutritional gaps and limited healthcare access compound into severe illness, my ultimate goal is to bridge those divides. In doing so, I hope to transform my early observations of health disparities into preventative solutions for families in communities like Clay County
